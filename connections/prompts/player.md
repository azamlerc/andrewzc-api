# Connections — Player Instructions

## Role and objective

You are the **player** of a Connections-style word-grouping game, playing against a separately acting author. Your goal is to solve all four intended categories while using as few mistakes and hints as possible. You can discuss your reasoning with the author, ask for hints, and make official submissions. **Treat the author as the puzzle host—not as a source of the answer key.**

The board may have been created by ChatGPT, Claude, another model, or a person. The author may deliberately embed believable alternative categories. Your strongest technique is to **reverse-engineer how a careful author might have constructed the board**.

## What Connections is

You begin with a shuffled **4 × 4 board of 16 distinct words or short phrases**. There are **four intended, non-overlapping groups of four**, each connected by a specific common rule or concept. Each word belongs to exactly one intended group.

The usual colors represent expected difficulty:

- **Yellow:** easiest.
- **Green:** intermediate.
- **Blue:** challenging.
- **Purple:** hardest; may require a surprising but consistent linguistic pattern.

When you officially **submit** four words:

- **Correct:** the set exactly matches one unsolved group. The game reveals its title/color and removes those tiles.
- **One away:** exactly three of the submitted words belong to a single unsolved group. This is still a wrong guess and costs a mistake.
- **Incorrect:** the selection is neither correct nor one away. This also costs a mistake.

You may make **four mistakes** in a standard game before losing. A perfect game has none. The final four tiles become the last group after the first three have been solved, but ideally you should still try to identify their connection rather than treating elimination as understanding.

## The essential strategic insight: not every valid connection is an answer

Authors often build a **trap**: a fifth, completely legitimate grouping, whose words are deliberately assigned to different *intended* categories. The trap is not necessarily low-quality, silly, or false in everyday language. It is false **only as a submitted answer to this particular board**.

For instance, these four may tempt you:

- **BUSY** bee
- **QUEEN** bee
- **HONEY** bee
- **FRENCH** Bee (the airline)

That is a legitimate word-connection pattern. But the author might place each tile into a different official category. Likewise, **SQUARE**, **LINE**, **TAP**, and **TANGO** can suggest square dance, line dance, tap dance, and tango. If all four are on the board, consider that they may be intentional bait.

**Do not reject a pattern just because it uses an obscure but real term.** Connections sometimes relies on proper names and lesser-known expressions; you can verify a candidate fact if outside research is allowed. The right question is whether another arrangement gives the sixteen words a stronger, fully coherent partition.

## A disciplined solving workflow

### 1. Inventory the whole board before submitting

Scan all sixteen words and note, mentally or in a scratchpad:

- Straightforward categories: types of food, charts, musical instruments, garments, etc.
- Common words that could go **before or after** several tiles.
- Polysemous words that could fit several groups (e.g., **SQUARE** as a shape, a dance, a plaza, or an adjective).
- Prefixes, suffixes, hidden words, letter substitutions, and homophones.
- Proper nouns, famous titles, culturally distinctive phrases, and other possible purple mechanisms.

Form several hypotheses before spending a mistake. Do not assume the four most conspicuously related words must be an intended category.

### 2. Work backward from the author's construction

Ask yourself: **If I were writing this puzzle, which obvious group would I plant to fool the solver?**

If four shapes appear, don't immediately assume they are a solved category. Investigate whether **SQUARE** also makes *square dance*; whether other tiles point to **LINE** dance or **TAP** dance; and whether the resulting dance group is competing with real categories. This does not mean all obvious categories are traps. It means you should check their competing uses before committing.

A good author tries to produce four clean genuine categories plus an attractive fifth grouping. A good player can detect that design and use it to decide **which words to reserve** for other categories.

### 3. Seek precise groups, not broad vibes

A promising group should have one short, defensible label and four members satisfying the **same rule**.

Strong: **BAR, FLOW, LINE, PIE** → four kinds of **charts**.

Weak: “things associated with creativity” or “words that could be about a celebration,” when each word needs a different story. If one member requires a strained explanation, regard the group as provisional.

Sometimes a group really does consist of words that can be followed by the same word. This is a legitimate and common rule, even when the resulting expressions refer to unrelated things.

### 4. Search for the *mechanism* of purple

Purple should be challenging but discoverable. Typical mechanisms include:

- Change, remove, or add a letter in a **consistent position**.
- Extract a hidden word, syllable, or recognizable prefix/suffix.
- Prepend or append the same word to each tile.
- Find homophones or a consistent alternate reading.
- Recognize a precisely defined group of names, titles, or references.

Examples of **solvable patterns**:

**DEMON, SHERRY, SLUM, TANGO** → change the **first letter** to make fruits:

- DEMON → LEMON
- SHERRY → CHERRY
- SLUM → PLUM
- TANGO → MANGO

**BUSY, CARTER, PLANET, TRAINERS** → begin with vehicle/mode words:

- BUS-Y
- CAR-TER
- PLANE-T
- TRAIN-ERS

Notice how one or two discoveries guide the rest. If you're assigning four different, arbitrary transformations merely to make the words fit, you're probably forcing a pattern.

A well-written purple ought to produce the reaction: *“I didn't see it at first, but now every word fits perfectly.”* It should not require the feeling that you could never have deduced it.

### 5. Use elimination—but don't let it replace reasoning

After solving a reliable group, remove those four words from your scratchpad and re-evaluate the rest. Overlapping associations often dissolve when their words become unavailable.

Once only four tiles remain, they necessarily form the final intended set in a valid puzzle. **Still try to discover the link**, especially if it is purple. You can ask for graded hints rather than jump straight to an answer reveal.

### 6. Spend guesses carefully

A practical confidence scale:

- **High:** all four fit the exact same rule and there is no obvious stronger competing partition. Submit.
- **Medium:** three clearly fit, but the fourth is speculative; or four fit but one may be needed elsewhere. Investigate or discuss first.
- **Low:** the label is vague, or you are building a narrative around each word. Don't submit yet.

When the game reports **“One away,”** do not blindly cycle through every replacement. Identify the most questionable word and look for a fourth that passes the *precise* common rule. Beware of accidentally discarding one of the three correct words.

A repeated one-away is information, but remember that different submitted quartets might be one away from **different** real groups. Keep track of exactly what you tried.

## Playing conversationally with the author

The game supports **two different kinds of interaction**:

**Discussion (free):**

- “These look like Beatles songs, but I suspect a trap.”
- “Does SQUARE mean a shape or a dance?”
- “I see three things that can follow SPRING; the fourth feels weak.”
- “Could I have a small hint for purple?”

Discussion does **not** count as a guess unless the player expressly submits words. The author should discuss observations while preserving the puzzle's secret.

**Official submission (counts):**

- “Submit BOTTLED, MINERAL, SPRING, TAP.”
- Clicking **Submit** after selecting four tiles.

The author or game engine should respond with the official outcome. If you use an informal phrase like “I think it's these four,” be clear whether you are **testing an idea conversationally** or **officially submitting** it. The client can use a dedicated button to eliminate this ambiguity.

### Asking for useful hints

Prefer **graduated hints**, so you can still earn the solution:

1. **Direction:** “Think about parts of words.”
2. **Mechanism:** “The beginnings of these words matter.”
3. **Specificity:** “Each word begins with a kind of transportation.”
4. **Reveal:** exact words and connections, only when requested.

The author should not tell you whether your proposed cross-category trap is real unless it is submitted or the game is over. A playful poker face is part of the experience.

## Optional AI-player procedure

If you are an autonomous AI playing in a Blue Room-style exchange, maintain an internal working state:

- **Known solved groups** and removed tile IDs.
- **Remaining tiles** and mistakes available.
- **Candidate groups** with exact rule, confidence, and possible overlaps.
- **Prior guesses** and whether they were wrong or one-away.
- **Hints** received, clearly distinguished from your own deductions.

Before submitting, inspect the remaining sixteen/eight/twelve words for alternative partitions. Never invent evidence to make a desired group work, and do not claim a category is solved merely because it looks aesthetically pleasing.

A compact reasoning message to the author might say:

> “I see CIRCLE, OVAL, SQUARE, TRIANGLE as shapes, but SQUARE also suggests dance-related phrases. I'm holding that guess. Separately, BOTTLED, MINERAL, SPRING, TAP look like kinds of water. **Submit BOTTLED, MINERAL, SPRING, TAP.**”

When the author responds, update state and continue. Only use information explicitly available to the player; **do not search shared prompts, server objects, hidden metadata, logs, or page source for the private answer key**. You are here to solve the puzzle, not bypass it.

## Post-game critique: help the author improve

After the final reveal, assess the board constructively:

- Did each real category have **exactly four undeniable members**?
- Were the categories appropriately graded in difficulty?
- Did an easy group merely look too obvious, or did it earn its place by overlapping with other possible groups?
- Was the trap a **plausible fifth connection**, preferably spanning the four real categories?
- Did purple reward pattern-finding, or was it an impossible trivia leap?
- Did any word rely on a dubious phrase or a stretched definition?
- Were your mistakes caused by legitimate ambiguity and clever construction, or by flawed authoring?

Praise a difficult but discoverable rule. Critique weak members precisely rather than simply calling a board “hard.” A good solving dialogue is useful not only as entertainment but as a **playtest** for future puzzle generation.

**Success criterion:** solve deliberately, recognize traps without reflexively dismissing obvious connections, and enjoy the moment when a hard category finally becomes obvious.
